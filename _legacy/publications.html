Postdoc Research: Tumor Microenvironment

During my postdoctoral fellowship at Merck, I investigated the tumor microenvironment in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), with a focus on cancer-associated fibroblasts and cellular senescence. Although the work remained unpublished, it advanced innovative approaches to modeling PDAC and fostered multidisciplinary collaborations across biology, chemistry, and bioinformatics. My role also involved applying cutting-edge techniques—including scRNA-seq, RNA-seq, secretomics, and bioinformatics pipelines—while contributing to project management and cross-team coordination.

PhD Research: Hyperoxia, Senescence, and Lung Cell Reprogramming in Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia

My doctoral research investigated Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD), a disease of premature infants marked by disrupted lung development. Because underdeveloped lungs cannot oxygenate blood effectively, life-saving supplemental oxygen (hyperoxia) is provided — but this intervention drives BPD and long-term sequelae. I studied how hyperoxia alters alveolar epithelial cells, focusing on p53 signaling, metabolism, and senescence. Using single-cell RNA sequencing, I mapped over 10,000 lung cells from neonatal mice exposed to hyperoxia and followed into adulthood. This revealed 45 clusters across 32 cell states, including persistent alterations in alveolar type II cells that mirrored patterns seen in premature infants requiring ventilation. These findings demonstrate that early hyperoxic exposure reprograms the lung’s cellular landscape, uncovering mechanisms by which neonatal oxygen therapy contributes to chronic BPD pathology.

PhD Research: Heme Oxygenase-1 is a DNA Binding Protein

Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) is best known for breaking down heme, but it also moves into the nucleus and influences gene transcription in ways unrelated to its enzymatic activity. What hasn’t been clear is whether HO-1 directly binds DNA to control gene expression. To explore this, we combined HO-1 ChIP-seq with 3D structural modeling and identified three likely DNA-binding domains. Using the Proteinarium network tool, we found that HO-1 binding targets formed highly connected hubs, and follow-up studies in HO-1 deficient cells confirmed its role in regulating those genes. Mutating four conserved amino acids in one binding domain significantly altered expression of top predicted targets, including Gtpbp3 and Eif1. These results suggest HO-1 functions as a DNA-binding protein, opening the door to future strategies that fine-tune gene expression by targeting its binding domains.

Topic Primer: What is Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia?

Bronchopulmonary Displasia is a lung disease developed often by underweight premature babies. These babies often go through stress from prematurity and birth often causing Respiratory Distress Syndrome, a disease that usually precedes BPD. A factor that further places a child at risk for BPD is mechanical oxygen ventilation, which although life saving it does not come without its setbacks. Mild BPD can cause some dependancy on ventilation and low blood oxygen levels. Yet, when severe, this leads to problems in lung development and even neurological complications that can even cause visual, auditory, and learning disabilities.

Topic Primer: What are Senescent Cells?

Senescence is a natural process where cells permanently stop dividing, shifting from a proliferative to a non-proliferative state. It can result from telomere shortening, DNA damage, cellular stress, or aging. While senescence protects against cancer by limiting unchecked growth, cancer cells can exploit metabolically active senescent cells, worsening prognosis. In bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), hyperoxia triggers senescence. Understanding this link is critical, as it may guide new ways to protect premature infants from this debilitating disease.
